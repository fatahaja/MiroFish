## 1. Program Overview

**Makan Bergizi Gratis (MBG)** — translated as "Free Nutritious Meals" — is the flagship social welfare and public health program of Indonesian President **Prabowo Subianto**, who took office on October 20, 2024. The program is the most expensive and ambitious social program in Indonesia's post-Reformasi era, with a stated goal of reducing child stunting, improving national human capital, and building food sovereignty.

MBG was formally launched on **January 6, 2025**, simultaneously across multiple provinces in Indonesia. The program provides free, nutritionally balanced meals to school-age children, toddlers (balita), pregnant women, and breastfeeding mothers. It is overseen by a newly created state body, **Badan Gizi Nasional (BGN)**, the National Nutrition Agency.

---

## 2. Key Objectives

- **Reduce stunting**: Indonesia has one of the highest stunting rates in Southeast Asia. As of 2023, approximately 21.5% of children under five were stunted (SSGI 2023). The MBG program targets reducing this rate to below 10% by 2029.
- **Improve school attendance and learning outcomes**: Hunger is a documented barrier to educational performance. The program aims to increase concentration, attendance, and retention.
- **Stimulate local food economy**: Meals are to be sourced from local farmers, fishermen, and food enterprises (UMKM), with priority given to local produce over imports.
- **Tackle malnutrition among vulnerable groups**: Beyond schoolchildren, the program specifically targets pregnant women and breastfeeding mothers, who are disproportionately affected by malnutrition in rural and eastern Indonesia.

## 3. Budget and Scale

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| **2025 Budget Allocation** | Rp 71 trillion (~USD 4.3 billion) |
| **Cost per meal (target)** | Rp 10,000 – Rp 15,000 per person per meal (varies by region) |
| **Target Beneficiaries (full-scale)** | ~82.9 million people |
| **Phase 1 (Jan 2025)** | ~3 million beneficiaries across selected provinces |
| **Full rollout target** | End of 2025 – beginning of 2026 |
| **Meals per school day** | 1 meal per day (lunch or breakfast, depending on school) |
| **Governing Body** | Badan Gizi Nasional (BGN) — National Nutrition Agency |

The Rp 71 trillion budget for 2025 was secured through a combination of reallocation from the national budget (APBN), including significant cuts to the education infrastructure and social assistance budgets. Critics noted that funding was partially drawn from the **Indonesia Endowment Fund for Education (LPDP)**, sparking debate among academics and civil society.

## 4. Key Stakeholders and Entities

### Government & Political

- **President Prabowo Subianto** — initiator and primary political champion; MBG is his signature campaign promise from the 2024 presidential election
- **Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka** — son of former President Joko Widodo; active promoter of MBG, especially among youth demographics
- **Dadan Hindayana** — appointed head of Badan Gizi Nasional (BGN); leads national program coordination
- **Sri Mulyani Indrawati** — Minister of Finance; responsible for budget allocation; expressed concerns about fiscal sustainability of the program's full-scale rollout
- **Saifullah Yusuf (Gus Ipul)** — Minister of Social Affairs; coordinates delivery to vulnerable populations
- **Coordinating Ministry for Human Development and Culture (Kemenko PMK)** — oversees cross-ministry coordination
- **Ministry of Education and Culture** — coordinates implementation through school networks
- **Ministry of Health** — provides nutritional standards and monitoring
- **BUMN (State-Owned Enterprises)** — Bulog (food logistics), ID FOOD, and others involved in supply chains

### Program Delivery

- **Satuan Pelayanan Pemenuhan Gizi (SPPG)** — Nutrition Fulfillment Service Units; local kitchens set up under BGN to prepare and distribute meals
- **Local governments (provincial and district)** — provide land and co-funding for SPPG kitchens; degree of readiness varies greatly
- **School principals and teachers** — frontline coordinators for distribution within schools
- **Kodam / TNI (Military)** — in some regions, the Indonesian Armed Forces were tasked with logistics and distribution support
- **BRI (Bank Rakyat Indonesia)** — digital payment infrastructure for tracking and financial accountability

### Civil Society & Critics

- **UNICEF Indonesia** — provides technical nutritional standards; has cautiously welcomed the program while urging attention to dietary quality
- **World Food Programme (WFP)** — consulted on best practices from global school feeding programs
- **Indonesian Pediatric Society (IDAI)** — professional medical body; raised concerns about menu standardization and food safety protocols
- **Indonesian Teachers' Association (PGRI)** — raised concerns about administrative burden on teachers managing distribution
- **FITRA (Forum Indonesia untuk Transparansi Anggaran)** — NGO monitoring budget transparency; flagged risks of corruption in procurement
- **Wahana Lingkungan Hidup Indonesia (WALHI)** — environmental organization; raised concerns about food sourcing, packaging waste
- **Opposition political parties** — PKS, Nasdem factions; scrutinize budget allocations and potential for political patronage in procurement

### Economic Actors

- **Local farmers and fishermen** — intended primary food suppliers; program promotes local sourcing
- **SMEs / UMKM** — small and medium enterprises contracted to prepare and distribute meals
- **Food importers** — opposed by program rhetoric; some controversy over whether actual procurement ends up relying on imported ingredients (e.g., imported beef, processed food)
- **Logistics companies** — involved in cold-chain and distribution challenges, especially in eastern Indonesia (Papua, Maluku, NTT)

## 6. Program Design and Menu

- **Nutritional composition**: Each meal designed to meet ~35–40% of daily caloric and nutrient requirements for the target age group
- **Menu components**: Rice or local staple + protein source (egg, fish, chicken, tofu/tempeh) + vegetables + fruit (where available) + milk (select beneficiary categories)
- **Regional variation**: Menus adapted to local food culture (e.g., sago in Papua and Maluku, corn in East Nusa Tenggara)
- **Special provisions**: For pregnant women and toddlers, additional nutrient-dense supplements (iron, folic acid) to accompany meals
- **Packaging**: Biodegradable packaging encouraged; single-use plastic use criticized by environmental groups
- **Milk component**: Originally included daily milk; scaled back in some regions due to cost and cold-chain limitations

## 7. Controversies and Public Debate

### 7.1 Budget Sourcing and Fiscal Impact

- Critics, including economists at UI (Universitas Indonesia) and INDEF (Institute for Development of Economics and Finance), argued that reallocating LPDP and education budgets to fund MBG undermines long-term human capital investment.
- Questions raised about sustainability: the Rp 71 trillion 2025 budget is widely seen as insufficient for true full-scale rollout at Rp 15,000/meal/day for 82.9 million people (estimated true cost: Rp 400–500 trillion per year).
- Fears of pro-cyclical deficit spending that could pressure the rupiah.

### 7.2 Quality and Food Safety Concerns

- Reports of substandard food quality in early distribution: soggy rice, spoiled side dishes, missing components.
- A widely shared incident in March 2025 reported students at several schools receiving incomplete or low-quality meals, generating viral social media outrage.
- Indonesian Pediatric Society (IDAI) raised concerns that menus may not consistently meet WHO/Ministry of Health nutritional standards.
- Contamination incidents in early pilot programs prompted calls for tighter food safety oversight.

### 7.3 Logistical Challenges

- Indonesia's geography — over 17,000 islands, many with poor road infrastructure — makes distribution extremely challenging.
- Cold-chain infrastructure is inadequate in eastern Indonesia (Papua, Maluku, East Nusa Tenggara).
- School distribution management creates administrative overhead for teachers and principals.
- Military involvement in logistics raised eyebrows among civil society regarding militarization of civilian welfare programs.

### 7.4 Procurement Transparency and Corruption Risk

- FITRA and Indonesia Corruption Watch (ICW) raised alarms about centralized procurement potentially benefiting politically connected suppliers.
- Local SPPG kitchen operator selection process lacked clear public tender in some regions.
- Accusations that some UMKM contracts were awarded to companies with ties to ruling coalition politicians.

### 7.5 Import vs. Local Sourcing Debate

- Program rhetoric emphasizes local farmers and food sovereignty; reality shows that in some regions, imported frozen chicken, beef, and processed food were used due to availability and cost.
- Agricultural groups (farmers' unions) expressed disappointment that local suppliers were not consistently prioritized in procurement.
- Debate over whether the program genuinely boosts local food economy or primarily benefits larger food distributors.

### 7.6 Nutritional Efficacy Skepticism

- Some nutrition experts question whether a single government-prepared meal per day can meaningfully reduce stunting, which is caused by chronic multi-year malnutrition and requires comprehensive household-level interventions.
- UNICEF and WHO have noted that stunting reduction requires multi-pronged approaches including sanitation, maternal health, and poverty alleviation — not just meal provision.

## 8. Public Opinion Landscape

### Supporters

- **Working-class and rural families**: Widely supportive; reduce household food costs for school-age children.
- **Prabowo loyalists and Gerindra party base**: View MBG as historic fulfillment of campaign promise; symbol of a caring, nationalist state.
- **Teachers and school staff** (mixed): Some appreciate the positive effect on student energy and attendance; others frustrated by administrative burdens.
- **Pro-government media and influencers**: Frame MBG as transformative welfare breakthrough comparable to BLT (direct cash transfers) and KIP (free schooling cards) under Jokowi.

### Critics

- **Urban educated middle class**: Skeptical of implementation quality and fiscal prudence; active in social media criticism.
- **Opposition party supporters**: Question whether program is sustainable, or politically timed to boost Prabowo's approval ratings.
- **Academic and economist community**: Generally supportive of the goal, critical of the execution method and budget sourcing.
- **Food safety advocates**: Concerned about inadequate monitoring and quality control across tens of thousands of kitchens.
- **Environmental advocates**: Critical of packaging waste and unsustainable procurement practices.

### Social Media Dynamics

- Twitter/X and Instagram highly active with #MakanBergizi, #MBG, #MakanGratis hashtags.
- Viral posts frequently feature photos of low-quality or incomplete meal boxes — often shared by parents and students.
- Counter-narratives from government accounts and pro-Prabowo influencers emphasize success stories and happy children.
- TikTok: popular among students showing their daily MBG meals (both positive "unboxing" content and complaints).
- YouTube: long-form policy discussion and investigative journalism on procurement and logistics.
- Reddit (r/indonesia): nuanced, skeptical discussions; English-language international diaspora reactions.
